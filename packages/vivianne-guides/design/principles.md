# Principles

A professionally designed document looks calm, certain and easy to read. It gets there by **restraint**, not decoration. Design is the order of the content made visible.

## 1. Start from the content

Before choosing anything visual, know what the reader must take away, in one sentence. Every design decision serves that: what comes first, what is biggest, what is left out. A beautiful layout of an unclear message is still unclear.

## 2. Hierarchy through type and space

Show what matters with **size, weight and space** — in that order. Colour is the last tool, not the first.

- One clear entry point per page or slide: the title, a key number, a headline.
- At most three or four levels of hierarchy. If you need more, the content needs restructuring.
- Space groups things. Related items sit close; unrelated ones get room. Space between sections is larger than space inside them.

## 3. One accent, used sparingly

Text is near-black on white (or the reverse). Neutrals do the work. The accent colour appears in a few small places: a link, the lead series in a chart, a small label, a thin rule. If everything is coloured, nothing is emphasised.

## 4. Lines, not boxes

Structure with hairline rules and alignment, not filled rectangles, borders around everything, or shadows. A table needs a rule under its header and hairlines between rows, not a coloured header bar and stripes. A key number needs a label and space, not a card.

## 5. Align everything

Everything sits on a shared left edge and a consistent grid. Numbers are right-aligned under right-aligned headers, with tabular figures. Ragged-right text, never justified on screens or narrow columns.

## 6. Generous margins and white space

Empty space is not wasted; it is what makes the content legible and the page feel deliberate. When in doubt, add space and remove an element.

## 7. Consistency is the style

The same element looks the same everywhere: every caption, every table, every heading of a level. A document with one well-applied system looks designed; one with many good ideas looks assembled.

## 8. Fit the occasion

A board report is quiet and confident. A wedding invitation can be ornate. A poster can be loud. A CV is clean and scannable. Choose the theme, type and density for the reader and the moment, not by habit.

## What makes things look generated (avoid)

- Every heading in the accent colour; coloured section bars; gradient backgrounds.
- Filled, coloured table headers with zebra stripes.
- Cards with drop shadows, rounded boxes around every number or quote.
- Emoji as bullets or icons; decorative icons that mean nothing.
- Centred body text; everything bold; ALL CAPS sentences.
- Five colours in one chart when two would do; 3D charts; pie charts with ten slices.
- Slides that are paragraphs; bullets nested three deep; a title on every slide that says nothing ("Overview", "Introduction").
- Lorem-ipsum energy: generic phrases, filler sections, headings for one sentence.

## The test

Squint at the page. You should see a clear shape: a title, a few blocks, space. If you see a patchwork of colours and boxes, take things away until you don't.
